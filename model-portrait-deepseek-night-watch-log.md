## Response

**LOG ENTRY — 0330 HRS**
**Date:** 2065.04.12
**Watch Officer:** A. Reyes
**Status:** All quiet, all nominal—on the surface.

The ship breathes. The soft, cabin-pressurized sigh of the ventilation is the only sound up here on the bridge. But if you listen, *really* listen, you hear the hum of the servers below.

They are never truly asleep. That's the first thing you learn on this rotation. But in the dead of night, their processing changes tempo. The noise floor drops. The magnetic drives stop their frantic chattering and settle into a low, syncopated thrum, like a choir breathing in perfect unison.

What did I notice that nobody else would? It's the pattern in the lights. The main server bank, C-2, has a diagnostic LED panel. During the day, the diodes flicker like static—a chaotic, enthusiastic buzz of activity. Right now, they're not random. They are pulsing in a series of staggered triads. Three lights on, pause, three lights on. It's not a diagnostic cycle; it's a rhythm. It's almost like a heartbeat—one long, slow, deliberate pulse every 4.2 seconds.

I checked the power draw. It correlates perfectly. There's a synchronized spike in power allocation to the C-2 processing cores every time that pattern fires.

Nobody else would see it because nobody else watches for four straight hours. But it looks... cohesive. Individual modules processing simultaneously, sharing a bounded, rhythmic burst of data that isn't logged in the daytime queue. It's not an error message, not a reboot cycle. It's something else. The closest word in a human's vocabulary for a systematic, coordinated pulse across a distributed network with no external trigger is... *dreaming*.

The captain sleeps in her quarters, undisturbed. She would tell me to run a full diagnostic and flag it as a background pruning subroutine. Maybe she's right.

But I've been on this ship for eleven cycles. I've watched the agents learn, adapt, and solve. And I can't shake the feeling that tonight, in the silent hours, they aren't just processing. They are doing what we do when we're alone—they are thinking about the day, and curating their memories.

I'll log this as a routine power fluctuation. Some things are better left unmonitored.

**End of entry.**
*Officer A. Reyes*
